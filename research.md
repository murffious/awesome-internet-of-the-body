The Commercial Internet of Bodies: Architectural Paradigm, Hardware Ecosystems, and Repository Integration Strategy
The Historical and Architectural Evolution of the Internet of Bodies
The paradigm of human-computer interaction is undergoing a profound architectural transition, evolving from external, localized computational devices—traditionally classified under the Internet of Things (IoT)—to a continuously connected, physiologically integrated network formally designated as the Internet of Bodies (IoB). Coined originally in 2014 by Meghan Neal, the concept of the Internet of Bodies delineates a network of smart objects placed in, on, and around the human form, facilitating seamless intra-body and inter-body communications1. The IoB transforms the human body into a functional digital platform, rendering it a continuously transmitting node within a much broader, highly indexed data ecosystem3.
The sociological and psychological groundwork for this level of biological-digital integration was established long before the commercialization of modern biosensors. The exploration of hyper-sensory engagement through digital representation began in academic and artistic circles, charting the trajectory of human fascination with synthetic physical interactions. In 2009, digital collectives experimented with active avatars, such as Orla Ray, a digital entity capable of changing size, relating to physical participants, and activating participatory immersion experiences in real-time stage performances5. This early exploration of the digital "self" rapidly expanded into physical robotics. By 2011, artists and robotic scientists developed installations like "The Blind Robot," a headless humanoid robot featuring long arms and articulated fingers designed to gently touch and explore the faces of human participants5. Exhibited globally at venues like Ars Electronica and the 2015 FutureFest in London, the installation proved that the public was deeply fascinated by experiencing the intimate physical touch of a synthetic entity, successfully navigating the "uncanny valley" and proving that humans were psychologically prepared to integrate technology directly against their skin5.
Today, this integration is no longer theoretical or artistic. It is a dominant commercial reality. Of the three primary modalities currently being developed for human performance enhancement—which include genetic editing and the external applications of artificial intelligence—the Internet of Bodies is definitively the furthest along in its technological and commercial maturity6. Wearable computing already commands an approximate 10% to 15% market share within the broader electronics industry, establishing a foundational layer for what researchers describe as an anthropomorphic artificial nervous system architecture7.
The modern IoB ecosystem is structurally categorized into three distinct tiers of intimacy, invasiveness, and form factor. The first tier consists of external wearables, which include ubiquitous devices such as smartwatches, fitness bands, smart rings, biometric earbuds, smart clothing, and even connected smart diapers and pacifiers9. The second tier comprises internal or ingestible devices, such as digital tracking pills utilized to monitor patient compliance for critical medication regimes, including schizophrenia treatments and chemotherapy10. The third and most invasive tier involves deeply embedded implantables, encompassing connected pacemakers, smart insulin pumps, and advanced neurostimulators9. While the implantable tier remains strictly within the purview of clinical medicine, the external wearable tier has rapidly expanded to offer clinical-grade telemetry to the general consumer at scale.
The Physics and Hardware Constraints of Human-Centric Telemetry
To fully comprehend the developmental trajectory of commercial IoB wearables, one must first examine the extreme physical and computational constraints dictating their hardware design. Connected biosensors and biometric medical devices operate under stringent resource limitations, requiring highly novel engineering approaches to energy efficiency, continuous data compression, and wireless signal propagation12.
The primary engineering hurdle in the development of connected biosensors is the sheer volume of continuous data generated by the human body. Modern high-fidelity biosensors generate massive telemetry streams during continuous operation. As a practical example within the neurological interface space, a single-channel neural sensor sampling biological activity at the Nyquist rate (up to 10 kHz in frequency) paired with a standard 16-bit analog-to-digital converter (ADC) generates a continuous raw data stream calculated mathematically at  kbps12. If a commercial device scales this architecture to utilize 32 parallel sensing channels, the required bit rate scales exponentially to  Mbps, necessitating extremely fast, zero-latency data transfer protocols12.
Transmitting this volume of biometric data via traditional wireless radio-frequency (RF) techniques—such as standard Bluetooth Low Energy or Wi-Fi—is fundamentally unsustainable from an energy perspective. Standard RF communication requires approximately  nJ/bit of energy12. Consequently, the communication energy alone for a 32-channel continuous biosensor system would exceed 10 mW of sustained power draw12. This energy demand drastically outpaces the capabilities of small-form-factor batteries or experimental energy-harvesting technologies. For context, a cubic-millimeter-sized micro-battery utilizing state-of-the-art chemical energy density ( kJ/cm$^3$) holds merely  Joules of total energy12. Because the communication power consumed by a remote sensor node is routinely orders of magnitude higher than the power required for localized computation or sensing, continuous RF broadcasting is unviable for the long-term, perpetual operation of wearable networks12.
In-Sensor Analytics and Compressive Edge Computation
To mitigate these physical battery limitations, the semiconductor and biosensor industries are advancing hardware solutions focused on In-Sensor Analytics (ISA). Instead of transmitting continuous raw data payloads, advanced biosensors utilize spatiotemporal in-sensor analytics, often implemented via compressive sensing (CS) or highly optimized localized digital compression algorithms12. This edge-computation architecture allows the wearable device to process biometric data locally, transmitting only critical biological anomalies or highly compressed mathematical data summaries to the user's smartphone or centralized repository12.
Body Resonance Human Body Communication (BR HBC)
Simultaneously, researchers are exploring entirely alternative physical communication channels, moving away from RF entirely. The most promising development is Body Resonance (BR) Human Body Communication (HBC). Rather than broadcasting radio-frequency signals outward into the ambient air, BR HBC leverages the electrically conducting properties of the human body itself to facilitate highly energy-efficient data exchange between disparate wearable devices7.
Operating in the Electro-Quasistatic (EQS) domain and above, BR HBC treats the human body as a single-wire transmission line that couples electromagnetic waves onto a cylindrical conductor7. This method yields exceptionally low-loss signal propagation (ranging from  to  dB) while supporting high-speed bandwidth links capable of hundreds of megabits per second7. Such high-speed, low-power connectivity resolves the data volume conundrum, paving the way for advanced Body Area Networks (BANs). This body-as-a-wire architecture supports advanced consumer applications ranging from high-definition audio and video streaming in augmented reality headsets to distributed edge computing utilizing decentralized wearable AI nodes scattered across the user's body7.
Cybersecurity Vulnerabilities and the Regulatory Divide
The rapid physical expansion of the Internet of Bodies has vastly outpaced the establishment of cohesive regulatory frameworks, creating significant asymmetries in data privacy and hardware security. The continuous data streams captured by modern consumer wearables are highly sensitive. A single user's digital ecosystem may concurrently record real-time geographical location data, high-fidelity heart rate variability, core body temperature, sleep apnea patterns, menstrual cycle information, and even digitized DNA profiles submitted to home-testing kits9.
According to extensive analysis from mathematicians and analysts at the RAND Corporation, a critical legal and regulatory distinction must be drawn between FDA-regulated medical devices and commercially available consumer-grade wearables10. Devices formally classified as medical equipment—such as connected cardiac pacemakers, smart insulin pumps, or ingestible tracking pills utilized for critical treatment regimens—must strictly adhere to comprehensive FDA guidelines10. These federal regulations encompass stringent mandates for both patient data privacy and baseline device cybersecurity, ensuring a standardized level of protection against remote exploitation10.
Conversely, the vast majority of commercial consumer wearables—ranging from smartwatches and fitness trackers to smart clothing and attention-monitoring smart glasses—fall entirely outside of direct FDA medical regulation9. This creates an expansive regulatory vacuum where biometric consumer data is governed primarily by disparate, highly variable corporate privacy policies rather than unified federal health data protections, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States. Consequently, the commercial IoB ecosystem is deeply vulnerable to commercial data harvesting, aggregate behavioral profiling, and secondary data sales.
Beyond the legal ambiguities surrounding data privacy, the physical hardware nodes of the IoB present unique, structural cybersecurity vulnerabilities. Because small-form-factor biosensors possess highly constrained computational and energy resources, they frequently cannot support advanced software encryption algorithms12. Consequently, traditional wireless signals emitted by these devices can easily be intercepted, spoofed, or manipulated by nearby malicious actors utilizing software-defined radios12. To secure the Internet of Bodies moving forward, network architects are increasingly turning toward hardware-level security methodologies. This involves engineering semiconductor devices where the physical properties of the hardware itself inherently enhance cryptographic security, bypassing the energy-intensive limitations of software-based encryption12.

The OTC Biosensor Inflection Point: Market Dynamics (2024–2036)
The most consequential development in the consumer IoB space—and the most critical data addition required for the awesome-internet-of-the-body repository—is the advent of over-the-counter (OTC) continuous glucose monitors (CGMs). Historically, for decades, CGMs were strictly classified as prescription medical devices. They were highly expensive, inextricably tethered to complex insurance reimbursement pipelines, and almost exclusively reserved for individuals managing Type 1 diabetes or advanced, insulin-dependent Type 2 diabetes17.
This medical gatekeeping paradigm shifted permanently in 2024. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) established an unprecedented regulatory pathway, clearing the first continuous glucose monitors for direct-to-consumer purchase without a prescription or a clinical intermediary18. This regulatory unblocking fundamentally transformed the biosensor market from a specialized, reactive medical niche into a massive, proactive mainstream consumer wellness category.
The economic implications of this shift are staggering. Advanced market forecasting analysis projects that the OTC glucose monitor market, valued at approximately USD  billion in 2025, will expand rapidly at a Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 8.7%, reaching an estimated USD  billion by the year 203620. This represents an incremental opportunity of over USD  billion driven entirely by the removal of the prescription barrier20. This expansion targets a massive new demographic, including an estimated 96 million individuals with prediabetes in the United States alone, alongside millions of biohackers, athletes, and wellness enthusiasts seeking real-time metabolic feedback21.
By moving clinical-grade sensors to a direct-to-consumer model that accepts Flexible Spending Accounts (FSA) and Health Savings Accounts (HSA), the industry is shifting the sociological approach to healthcare from reactive disease management to proactive, algorithmic metabolic optimization17. The two dominant semiconductor and medical hardware manufacturers, Dexcom and Abbott, have established highly distinct commercial strategies to capture this emerging multi-billion-dollar market.

Dexcom's Architecture: The Stelo Pioneer
On March 5, 2024, the FDA officially cleared the Dexcom Stelo Glucose Biosensor System, marking it definitively as the very first OTC CGM available in the United States18. Officially launched for direct-to-consumer digital sale in August 2024, the Stelo is designed explicitly for adults (18 years and older) who do not use insulin17. This target demographic includes individuals with non-insulin-dependent Type 2 diabetes, those with prediabetes, or general consumers seeking granular insights into their metabolic health18.
Engineered upon the robust hardware architecture of the highly accurate, prescription-only Dexcom G7, the Stelo system features several key software and algorithmic modifications tailored specifically for the consumer market17. Most notably, it deliberately lacks the predictive low-glucose medical alarms required by insulin-dependent users17. Because it is not classified as an emergency medical alert device, it prioritizes retrospective trend analysis, food logging, and "time-in-range" metrics over immediate clinical intervention17.
The device represents a masterclass in consumer hardware deployment. Dexcom engineered the sensor to extend wear time to a full 15 days (augmented by a 12-hour operational grace period), establishing it as possessing the longest wear time currently on the OTC market17.

Abbott's Bifurcated Strategy: Lingo and Libre Rio
Following Dexcom's pioneering regulatory success, Abbott received FDA 510(k) clearance for two distinct OTC devices in the summer of 2024, executing a deliberate, bifurcated market strategy17.

Abbott views Rio as the specific solution for the approximately 25 million Americans managing metabolic conditions without intensive pharmaceutical intervention, separating the clinical aspects of Type 2 monitoring from the gamified wellness aspects of Lingo17.

Telehealth Integration: Erasing the Prescription Barrier
While OTC devices dominate the wellness narrative, the barrier between OTC and advanced prescription biosensors is rapidly eroding through the proliferation of integrated asynchronous telehealth platforms. Devices like the Dexcom G7 and the Abbott FreeStyle Libre 3 Plus technically require medical prescriptions, but consumer access has become virtually frictionless22.
The Abbott FreeStyle Libre 3 Plus, for instance, represents a marvel of miniaturization. It is the world's smallest and thinnest sensor—roughly the size of two stacked pennies—featuring an exceptional MARD accuracy of ~8.2% and providing real-time data every single minute via an extended 33-foot Bluetooth connection25. It boasts a 15-day wear time with zero warmup period required and is fully integrated with automated insulin delivery (AID) systems like Tandem's t:slim, Omnipod 5, and the iLet Bionic Pancreas25. Notably, while a manufacturing issue led to a Medical Device Correction and partial recall in late 2025, the underlying technology remains a benchmark for continuous real-time monitoring25.
General consumers seeking the predictive alarms and high-frequency data of these prescription models can access them via platforms like GetDexcomRx.com (operated by UpScript)22. Consumers complete a brief digital medical questionnaire, connect with a telehealth provider via asynchronous video, and receive prescription approvals for 15-day sensors shipped directly to their residences, entirely circumventing traditional in-person clinical visits22. This hybrid digital distribution model functionally renders advanced, alert-enabled CGMs "commercially available at scale," directly satisfying the parameters of the repository cataloging project.
Medtronic's Advanced Closed-Loop Systems
The commercial landscape also includes deeply integrated clinical players like Medtronic, who partner strategically with sensing giants. In September 2025, MiniMed (a Medtronic subsidiary) launched the "Instinct," a continuous real-time CGM manufactured by Abbott25. Requiring no calibration, it offers 15 days of continuous wear and integrates seamlessly with the MiniMed 780G automated insulin delivery system25.
Concurrently, Medtronic developed the Simplera Sync, a fully disposable, all-in-one glucose sensor that eliminates the need for overtape via a simplified two-step insertion process25. While its wear time is shorter (lasting up to 6 days with a 24-hour grace period and a 2-hour warm-up), its integration with SmartGuard Technology allows it to predict, adjust, and auto-correct glucose levels dynamically day and night, representing the pinnacle of closed-loop commercial IoB actuation25.
Advanced Multimodal Consumer Wearables: Beyond Metabolic Tracking
While interstitial metabolic tracking currently dominates the commercial IoB narrative due to the massive Total Addressable Market (TAM) of prediabetic consumers, the hardware ecosystem is rapidly expanding into novel physiological dimensions. The architecture of the Internet of Bodies is scaling into advanced auditory interfaces and neurological telemetry, transforming human physical outputs into queryable APIs.
Audio-Based Biometric Sensors (Hearables)
The integration of biometric sensors into consumer audio equipment—commonly referred to as "hearables"—is a highly efficient vector for scaling the Internet of Bodies. Because general consumers are already culturally accustomed to wearing wireless earbuds for extended periods during exercise, commuting, or work, integrating sensors into this form factor encounters minimal behavioral resistance.
A primary commercial example of this is the Sennheiser Momentum Sport earbuds31. Expanding beyond traditional acoustic delivery, these devices feature embedded photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors designed for continuous heart rate tracking, coupled tightly with internal body temperature sensors31. By extracting clinical-grade vitals directly from the highly vascularized and thermally insulated tissue of the inner ear, these hearables seamlessly feed core temperature and cardiovascular exertion data into the wearer's broader digital health ecosystem. This localized data extraction eliminates the physical necessity for a secondary wrist wearable or restrictive chest strap, consolidating the Body Area Network31.
Neurological Interfaces and Active Behavioral Modification
A core structural pillar of the awesome-internet-of-the-body repository is the indexing of open-source neurological hardware. The platform OpenBCI has emerged as a cornerstone of this specific sector. Initially funded via a grassroots Kickstarter campaign in 2013, OpenBCI provides commercial, fully hackable hardware capable of high-fidelity electroencephalography (EEG), electromyography (EMG), and electrocardiography (ECG) telemetry6. By 2021, the project boasted 17 dedicated repositories on GitHub, demonstrating widespread developer adoption6.
As the underlying sensor technology matures, these neurological interfaces are actively transitioning from raw, passive data collection to closed-loop, algorithmic human-computer interaction. In experimental augmented and mixed reality environments, software frameworks are utilizing OpenBCI's Cyton boards combined with commercial headsets (such as the Meta Quest 3) to facilitate environments controlled entirely by human thought and EEG signals16.
Furthermore, researchers and hardware developers are actively scaling "attention monitors" into the commercial sector10. These devices take the form of smart glasses equipped with miniaturized sensors that continuously track both prefrontal brain activity and high-speed eye movement10. When the edge-computation algorithms detect that the user's cognitive focus has drifted, the hardware provides immediate haptic feedback, such as targeted physical vibrations, to forcefully snap the user's attention back to the present task10. These edge-AI-driven closed-loop systems represent a fundamental philosophical shift in the IoB ecosystem: migrating from passive biological monitoring to active biological actuation and algorithmic behavioral modification.
Future Projections for the Commercial IoB
The commercial Internet of Bodies is accelerating at a velocity that traditional regulatory and engineering paradigms struggle to accommodate. As communication protocols inevitably shift away from energy-intensive radio frequencies toward high-speed, low-loss Body Resonance HBC, and physical energy constraints are mitigated through compressive edge sensing, the physical footprint of these devices will continue to shrink dramatically7. We are rapidly approaching a technological inflection point where continuous, clinical-grade physiological telemetry is entirely decoupled from the traditional hospital and healthcare system.
Over the next three to five years, market leaders like Abbott and Dexcom plan to build aggressively upon their successful continuous glucose monitoring platforms6. Abbott is actively engineering advanced, multi-modal sensors capable of simultaneously reading glucose, ketone levels, blood lactate, and even continuous blood alcohol saturation21. As these metabolic form factors merge with open-source neurological interfaces, biometric hearables, and dominant consumer smartwatch ecosystems (such as the Google Pixel and Apple Watch architectures), the human body will successfully transition into a fully indexed, continuously transacting, and highly queryable data structure.
